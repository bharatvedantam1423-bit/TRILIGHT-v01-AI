**What is it?**
A gated, funded journey that takes a NIAT student’s Physical AI, robotics or hardware idea from a first question
to a complete, qualified product that real customers pay for and an outside team can build again.

**Why “TRL8”?**
On the Technology Readiness Level scale created at NASA and used across industry, TRL 8 means a system that is
complete and qualified. That is our finish line, so the program is named after it. Written TRL8, pronounced
“Trilight”.

**Why “Trilight”?**
The finish line has three lights, and all three must be green: Build (a repeatable build an outside team can

All NIAT students. No hardware experience is needed to start. Teams with an existing TRL 4 prototype can

Brand and student learning outcomes.

**What do partners get?**
A real problem worked on by an accountable team, early access to proven hardware talent, and rights agreed case

## 1. The Idea

Every year, thousands of students build something that works once, in a lab, for a demo. Then what? TRL8 exists
to make sure that moment is not where the story ends, but where it starts.

TRL8 is where a hardware, robotics, IoT or Physical AI idea stops being a class project and starts being a
product: engineered, tested, and proven in the world it is actually meant for, not the world of a classroom bench.

It is a journey, not a course. A student enters with curiosity or a rough idea. They leave with a product that
works, sells, and can be built again at scale, and the evidence to prove all three.

> **What we mean by Physical AI**
> AI that senses and acts in the physical world: robots, drones, smart machines and sensor systems. The AI itself
> can run on the device, or as software on a phone or in the cloud. What makes it physical is that it senses the
> real world through hardware.

## 2. The Name: What TRL8 Means

Written **TRL8**. Pronounced **“Trilight”**. The program is named after its finish line.

### What “TRL” means

TRL stands for Technology Readiness Level. It is a nine-step scale, first developed at NASA in the 1970s and later
adopted by the US Department of Defense, the European Space Agency and the European Union, that measures how
close a technology is to real use. TRL 1 is an idea on paper. TRL 9 is a system proven through real, sustained
operation.

Because the scale is shared across industry, it gives engineers, managers, customers and investors one common
language. A student who says “we are at TRL 6” is speaking the language their future employers and customers
already use.

### Why 8

On that scale, TRL 8 means the actual system is complete and qualified through test and demonstration. That is
exactly what our top stage asks for: a complete, qualified system that customers are paying for, with a build
process good enough for an outside team to produce a second unit. Every team knows from its first day where the
journey ends.

### Why not 9

TRL 9 means a system proven through successful operation over time. No review panel can award that. The market
does, over years, after a team leaves the program. TRL8 takes a team to the point where that test can begin. We
stop at 8 because 9 is not taught or reviewed. It is earned.

### Why “Trilight”

Trilight is how TRL8 is said, and it describes what the finish line looks like: three lights that must all be
green at the same time. The three lights are the tagline, Build • Test • Deploy, turned into a standard.

| Light | What it proves | Evidence at TRL 8 |
|---|---|---|
| Build | It can be built again | A repeatable build process (SOP, BOM, QA) good enough for an outside team to build a second unit |
| Test | It is complete and qualified | The full system passes its qualification tests in its intended environment |
| Deploy | People are paying for it | Multiple paying customers, or a signed scale contract |

The same idea runs through every gate below TRL 8: a gate is a light that turns green on evidence, never on a date.

## 3. The Problem It Solves

Most student hardware and robotics projects do not fail for lack of talent. They fail for three specific,
nameable reasons:

- **No real deployment site.** A prototype that only ever runs in a classroom never meets real dust, vibration,
  lighting, or an impatient real user.
- **No reliability discipline.** Nobody grades whether a system works twice, only whether it worked once, on demo
  day.
- **No funding tied to proof.** Money is given up front or not at all, never released in response to evidence that
  the system got harder to break.

Hardware makes these failures unforgiving in a way software does not. A slide deck can bluff its way through a
pitch. A robot arm cannot bluff its way through a field test.

TRL8 is designed to remove all three failure modes at once, not by teaching more, but by building the conditions
that force an idea to survive contact with reality.

| Failure mode | How TRL8 removes it |
|---|---|
| No real deployment site | From TRL 5 onward, every team works at a real host site with real users, and field milestones are confirmed by that site. |
| No reliability discipline | Every Execution gate is judged on field evidence, including logged field-reliability data by TRL 7. TRL 8 requires a build process good enough for an outside team to build a second unit. |
| No funding tied to proof | Grants are released only when a Technology Readiness Review signs off a gate. |

## 4. What TRL8 Actually Is

> A gated, funded journey that takes a student’s Physical AI or hardware idea from a first question to a complete,
> qualified product that real customers pay for and an outside team can build again, validated at each step in a
> real operating environment, not a lab.

Three commitments sit underneath that definition, and none of them bend.

### 1. Mandatory embodiment

Every project must close a real sense–decide–act loop, demonstrated end to end by TRL 4:

| Step | What it must be |
|---|---|
| Sense | The physical world, measured through hardware the team builds or integrates: sensors, cameras, meters and similar. This part is mandatory. |
| Decide | AI. It can run on the device, or as software on a phone, a server or the cloud. |
| Act | A physical action (a motor, a valve, an alarm), or a recommendation delivered to a person through a dashboard or app. |

So a dashboard or recommendation engine qualifies as the “act” when it is driven by the team’s own physical
sensing. A pure software project with no physical sensing does not qualify, and could not clear the PCB and
enclosure requirements at TRL 5 and TRL 6 anyway.

| Qualifies? | Example |
|---|---|
| Yes | Soil-moisture sensors across a farm feed a cloud AI model that recommends irrigation times on the farmer’s phone. |
| Yes | A robot arm sorts parts on a conveyor using on-device computer vision. |
| Yes | Wearable sensors stream data to an app whose AI flags unsafe lifting posture for factory workers. |
| No | A chatbot, or a dashboard built only on public datasets, with no sensing hardware of the team’s own. |

### 2. Evidence-gated funding

Money is released only when a Technology Readiness Review confirms a stage is genuinely cleared, never because a
calendar date arrived.

### 3. Real problems, real customers

Every team works on a real problem, taken from the TRL8 problem bank or brought as the students’ own idea. From
TRL 5 onward, every team works at a real site with real users, and by TRL 7, with a real customer who pays.

## 5. How the Journey Works

TRL8 has two doors in, and one ladder once you are inside.

```
 Explorer │ TRL 1-2   TRL 3   TRL 4 │ TRL 5   TRL 6   TRL 7   TRL 8
PRE-ENTRY │   FOUNDATION · TRL 1-4  │     EXECUTION · TRL 5-8
```

### Who can join

- **Open to all NIAT students.** No prior hardware experience is needed to enter through the Standard Door.
- **Individual first, then teams.** Explorer is completed individually. Teams of 3–5 students form at TRL 1-2 and
  climb the ladder together.
- **Your problem, or ours.** Teams take a problem from the TRL8 problem bank or bring their own idea. Either way,
  the problem must be real and the project must meet the embodiment rule (Section 4).
- **Participation is optional and carries no fee.**

### The Standard Door: earning your way in

Every student starts with a structured funnel. At Explorer they learn the basics of hardware and AIoT and clear
checkpoints on them. At TRL 1-2 they form a team that learns how to build a proposal and submits one, against a
problem from the problem bank or an idea of its own. An AI-scored evaluation decides whether the team has earned
entry, scoring every proposal on the same parameters with a written justification for each.

This is not paperwork. It is the first real test of whether a team is ready, and clearing it should feel like an
achievement.

### The Fast-Track Door: proving your way in

A team that already has a working project at TRL 4 or above (a breadboard-tested lab prototype that closes the
sense–decide–act loop) skips the Standard Door entirely. A one-time review confirms the claim and places the team
on the stage its evidence supports. No retroactive coursework is required.

> **Fast-Track in practice**
> A team arrives with a breadboard prototype that the review confirms meets the TRL 4 standard. It enters at TRL 5
> and receives the TRL 5 grant of ₹70,000.

### Once inside: two phases

**FOUNDATION (TRL 1-4)** — Structured learning and building, together. From Explorer to TRL 3, every gate needs a
skill proven (a Checkpoint) and something built (a proposal, a simulation), never one standing in for the other.
Foundation ends at TRL 4, where the lab prototype itself is the proof.

**EXECUTION (TRL 5-8)** — No more curriculum, no more assessments. Progress is judged purely on field validation
and commercial proof, confirmed with the host site or the customer. This is where the real world becomes the
teacher.

### The ladder

Each stage lists what a team must prove to clear it. In Foundation the two kinds of evidence are a skill and a
build. In Execution they are business proof and technical proof.

**Foundation** — Skill (Checkpoint) · Build

| Stage | Skill (Checkpoint) | Build |
|---|---|---|
| Explorer | Hardware & AIoT 101 L1 and Computational Thinking L1 | Individual learning |
| TRL 1-2 | Problem-to-Pitch L1 | A proposal that passes the AI-scored evaluation |
| TRL 3 | Technology Deep Dive L1 and UI Engineering L1 | A working simulation |
| TRL 4 | — | Lab prototype, breadboard-tested, closing the sense–decide–act loop |

**Execution** — Business · Technical

| Stage | Business | Technical |
|---|---|---|
| TRL 5 | Validated in a real operating environment (a host site) | Proof of concept on Zero PCB, in an enclosure |
| TRL 6 | Tested with real users: 5 for B2B, 20 for B2C | Golden Sample: custom PCB and enclosure |
| TRL 7 | A real customer pays for it | Beta product built to the standards needed for certification |
| TRL 8 | Multiple paying customers, or a signed scale contract | System complete and qualified; repeatable build process (SOP, BOM, QA); a second unit buildable by an outside team |

*Table 1 — The ladder. A stage’s grant unlocks when a team enters it (Section 7).*

### How long it takes

Gates open on evidence, not dates, so there are no deadlines. For planning only, these are the ranges we expect a
committed team to need at each stage, alongside regular studies.

| Explorer | TRL 1-2 | TRL 3 | TRL 4 | TRL 5 | TRL 6 | TRL 7 | TRL 8 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 4–6 weeks | 4–6 weeks | 6–10 weeks | 2–3 months | 3–4 months | 3–6 months | 3–6 months | 6–9 months |

Taken together, a strong team could go from Explorer to TRL 8 in roughly one to two years. Products differ widely,
so these ranges will be refined as the first cohorts climb.

### When a team changes or stalls

- **Team changes.** Members can be added or replaced at any TRR, with panel approval, as long as the team stays at
  3–5 members.
- **Stalled teams.** If a team presents at no TRR for six months, the panel holds a check-in to agree a plan or
  close the journey. A closed team’s hardware returns to the lab and its unspent grant returns to the pool.

## 6. What Makes It Credible

### The compound gate

Nothing in TRL8 is unlocked by one kind of evidence alone. In Foundation, a team needs both a build milestone and
a proven skill. A worked example makes this concrete: to clear TRL 3 and move up to TRL 4, a team must pass the
simulation milestone and the C3 Checkpoint, the Checkpoint at the simulation stage. Clearing only one does not
move it forward.

In Execution the same discipline holds in a different shape: a team needs both field validation and commercial
proof. A working prototype nobody has paid for is not enough. A paying customer for something that does not
reliably work is not enough either.

TRL 4 is the bridge between the two phases and is judged on the prototype alone: the first time the full
sense–decide–act loop works end to end.

### Checkpoints: proving the skill

A Checkpoint is a learn-then-assess exam. Students first complete a learning module, such as Hardware & AIoT 101
L1, and then take an assessment built around it. Checkpoints are the skill half of every Foundation gate from
Explorer to TRL 3. (L1 means Level 1 of a module.)

The Checkpoint at the simulation stage, in TRL 3, is called the C3 Checkpoint.

### Technology Readiness Reviews: deciding the gate

Every gate is decided at a Technology Readiness Review (TRR), where a panel examines the team’s evidence and
decides whether the stage is cleared.

| | |
|---|---|
| When | Fixed monthly review windows, with a team self-review every 15 days. |
| Who | The Internal Team. |
| What teams bring | A gate-specific evidence pack (Annexure C). |
| What teams get | A written decision every time: what was accepted and, if the gate is not cleared, exactly what is missing. |
| If a gate is missed | The team fixes what is missing and presents again at the next window. There is no limit on attempts. |

> **Evidence, not attendance**
> Funding follows the same rule. A grant tranche is released only when a TRR confirms a gate is cleared, never
> because a set number of weeks has passed.

### Verification people can trust

- A review panel of faculty/instructors and industry mentors signs off on every gate.
- Every proposal is scored against the same parameters, with a written justification for each, not a black-box
  number.
- Field and commercial milestones are confirmed directly with the host site and the customer with a proof, not
  self-reported.
- A panel member connected to a team, its host site or its customer steps out of that team’s review.

### What we ask of mentors (internal)

Each mentor guides and meets each team at least twice a month (mostly virtually). Panel members review at the
monthly TRR windows.

## 7. Money, Miles and Incentives

### Grant tranches

TRL8 has a ₹3 Cr grant pool each year, released strictly against TRR sign-off, never on a calendar. A stage’s grant
unlocks when a team enters it, to fund the work of that stage. Grants are per team.

| Stage | Grant unlocked on entry (per team) | Bonus GRIT Miles on completion |
|---|---|---|
| Explorer | — | 0 |
| TRL 1-2 | — | 0 |
| TRL 3 | — | 20 |
| TRL 4 | ₹30,000 | 40 |
| TRL 5 | ₹70,000 | 80 |
| TRL 6 | ₹1,50,000 | 120 |
| TRL 7 | ₹2,50,000 | 200 |
| TRL 8 | No separate grant: remaining grant balance or the team’s own revenue | 400 |
| **Total, Explorer to TRL 8** | **₹5,00,000** | **860** |

*Table 2 — Grants and GRIT Miles*

The most a team can draw from TRL 4 through TRL 7 is ₹5 lakh. There is no separate TRL 8 grant: after TRL 7, teams
use any remaining balance of their ₹5 lakh or the revenue they have generated.

### How grant money moves

- **Spent, not handed over.** Grants are spent through the program against invoices, as vendor payments or
  reimbursements against bills. They are never paid as cash into students’ accounts.
- **Unspent money carries forward** within the team’s journey.
- **Hardware stays with the work.** Equipment bought with grant money stays with the team while it is active and
  returns to the lab if the team exits. The team’s unspent balance then returns to the pool.
- **What it can be spent on:** components, PCB fabrication, enclosures and 3D printing, tools and test-equipment
  hire. Not salaries, personal devices, or anything unrelated to the product.
- If the year’s pool runs low, grants are released in the order gates are cleared. A cleared team keeps its stage
  and is first in line when the next year’s pool opens.

### How far ₹3 Cr goes: an illustration

The real split depends on how many teams clear each gate. The table below is illustrative only. It assumes that in
a steady year about half the teams at each stage clear it.

| TRL 4 | 200 | ₹30,000 | ₹60,00,000 |
| TRL 5 | 100 | ₹70,000 | ₹70,00,000 |
| TRL 6 | 50 | ₹1,50,000 | ₹75,00,000 |
| TRL 7 | 20 | ₹2,50,000 | ₹50,00,000 |
| Subtotal | | | ₹2,55,00,000 |

At 3–5 students per team, 200 teams entering TRL 4 would mean roughly 600–1000 students building real prototypes in

- Bonus GRIT Miles are miles in NxtWave’s GRIT Program, awarded when a stage is completed (Table 2).
- Winning a national or international competition earns bonus miles as per GRIT Guidelines.

### Placement and internship pipeline

Active throughout the program, not just at the end: a direct channel into partner hardware companies for

### The annual Open/Demo Day

Once a year the Physical AI & Robotics Team runs an Open/Demo Day, where teams show what they are building to

something worth showing, from a breadboard prototype at TRL 4 to a product a customer already pays for at TRL 7,
so a team does not have to be finished to take part. Each team decides what it shows, because it owns the work.

## 8. What It Produces

### For the student

- A real, working hardware product with paying customers behind it, not just a certificate.
- Funding earned along the way, not handed out up front.
- A portfolio that can survive a technical interview, because it survived a field test first.
- Skills proven at every Foundation gate, and a written record of every gate cleared.
- An annual Demo Day, where their work is shown to industry partners, recruiters and the next cohort.

### For NxtWave

- **Brand.** NxtWave becomes known as the place where students ship real hardware products, measured on the scale
  industry already trusts.
- **Learning outcomes.** Every student who climbs the ladder has proven skills at each Foundation gate and has
  shipped work that survived a real site.

### What success looks like

We track these each year:

- Students entering Explorer, and teams clearing each gate.
- Products reaching TRL 7 (paying customers) and TRL 8 (qualified, repeatable, paid for at more than one customer).
- Internships and full-time roles in hardware companies.
- Competition wins.

### The product at the top of the ladder

**TRL 7** — A functional unit operating in the real host-site environment, with at least one paying customer and
logged field-reliability data. Still hand-built, not yet repeatable.

**TRL 8** — A complete, qualified system with multiple paying customers or a signed scale contract, and a
documented, repeatable build process (SOP, BOM, QA) good enough for an outside team to build a second unit.

TRL 7 proves someone will pay. TRL 8 proves it can be done again, at scale, for more customers.

## 9. Safety, Compliance and Risk

Hardware in the real world carries real risks. These are the ones stakeholders ask about most, and how TRL8 handles
each.

| Risk | How TRL8 handles it |
|---|---|
| Student safety in labs | A lab safety induction before any team builds at TRL 4. |
| Safety at host sites | No field deployment without a safety sign-off from the panel and from the host site, within a scope the host approves in writing. |
| Regulation: drones, wireless, product safety | The TRL 7 gate requires a beta product built to the standards needed for certification. The TRR checks the rules that apply to the product, such as India’s Drone Rules, 2021 for drones, WPC approval for wireless devices, and BIS registration for notified electronics. |
| Fake or token customers | A paying customer must be an arm’s-length buyer (not family, friends or NxtWave), paying at least the cost of the unit’s parts, backed by an invoice and confirmed by the panel. |
| Misuse of grant money | Grants are spent only against invoices. Equipment is tracked and returned to the lab if a team exits. |
| Stalled teams holding funds | Monthly TRR windows, and a check-in after six months without progress. |
| Academic overload | No deadlines, and indicative time ranges for planning. TRL8 carries no academic credit, so it never competes with coursework for grades. |
| A product failing at a customer site | A certification-ready beta before any sale at TRL 7, logged field-reliability data. |
| The pool running out | Grants released in the order gates are cleared, with cleared teams first in line the next year. |

> **A lesson from ideaForge (Annexure D)**
> Regulation can shape a market. Restrictions on drone use in India constrained ideaForge’s civilian operations in
> the mid-2010s. Checking compliance at the TRL 7 gate means a team learns what applies to its product before a
> customer does.

includes learning, assessment and proving the concept in simulation.

**Does every team member have to pass?**
At Explorer, yes. Beyond explorer any one team member needs to clear the Checkpoint individually.

**What happens when a team fails a gate?**
It receives written feedback on what is missing and can present it again at the next monthly TRR window, as many
times as needed.

**Is there any teaching in Execution?**
No curriculum or assessments. Mentors guide teams, and progress is judged on field and commercial proof.

**What is a TRR?**
A Technology Readiness Review: the gate review where a panel examines a team’s evidence and decides whether the
stage is cleared.

**How often do we meet, and who sits on the panel?**
Monthly TRR windows, with a team self-review every 15 days. The panel is the Internal Team.

**What evidence do teams bring?**
A gate-specific evidence pack. Annexure C lists what is required at each stage.

**How do we verify field and commercial claims?**
Directly with the host site and the customer: confirmation of deployments and users from the site, and invoices and
payment proof from the customer.

**What if I am connected to a team, its site or its customer?**
You step out of that team’s review.

**How much time does mentoring take?**
A mentor guides and meets each team at least twice a month, mostly virtually.

### NxtWave Sales

**How do we explain TRL8 to a parent in one line?**
“TRL8 is where your child’s project becomes a real product that real customers pay for, with funding released each
time they prove it works.” Lines for other audiences are in Section 2.

**What does Trilight mean?**
It is how TRL8 is said, and it names the finish line: three green lights for built, tested and deployed.

**How does TRL8 feed placements?**
Through a placement and internship pipeline into partner hardware companies that runs throughout the program, not
just at the end.

**What will recruiters see?**
A product that survived a real site, and a written record of each gate the student cleared and the evidence behind
it.

### Students

**Do I need hardware experience?**
No. The Standard Door starts with Hardware & AIoT 101.

**Is it optional? Does it cost anything?**
Participation is optional and there is no fee.

**Can I join alone?**
You complete Explorer on your own. From TRL 1-2, you work in a team of 3–5.

**Can I bring my own idea?**
Yes. Take a problem from the TRL8 problem bank or bring your own. It must solve a real problem and include real
physical sensing.

**My AI runs in an app or the cloud. Is that allowed?**
Yes. The AI can be software. What must be physical is the sensing.

**Is a software-only project allowed?**
No. Your project must sense the real world through hardware you build or integrate.

**I have already built something. Can I skip ahead?**
Yes, through the Fast-Track Door, if it is at TRL 4 or above: a breadboard-tested lab prototype that closes a
sense–decide–act loop. A one-time review places you on the stage your evidence supports.

**How much money does my team get, and how?**
A grant unlocks each time your team enters a funded stage: ₹30,000 at TRL 4, ₹70,000 at TRL 5, ₹1,50,000 at TRL 6
and ₹2,50,000 at TRL 7. It is spent through the program against invoices, not paid into your account.

**What are GRIT Miles?**
Miles in NxtWave’s GRIT Program. You earn bonus miles each time you complete a stage, from 20 at TRL 3 to 400 at
TRL 8, plus bonus miles for competition wins as per GRIT Guidelines.

**How long will it take?**
It depends on your product and your pace; Section 5 gives planning ranges. There are no deadlines, because gates
open on evidence, not dates.

**What if we fail a gate?**
You get written feedback, fix what is missing and present again at the next monthly review.

**Where do I find a real site and customer?**
Problem-bank partners are the first place to look for a host site. If you bring your own idea, mentors and the
partner network help you find one.

**Can I use my project in competitions like SIH?**
Yes. TRL8 funds entry and preparation for platforms such as iDEX, Smart India Hackathon, NIDAR, IRoC-U and the
Meher Baba Competition, and wins earn bonus GRIT Miles.

**Will this help me get placed?**
The placement and internship pipeline runs throughout the program, not only at the end.

**Will my project be shown to anyone?**
Yes, at the Demo Day held once a year, where industry partners, recruiters, faculty and students thinking about
joining all come to see the work. Your team decides what it shows, because you own it.

### Parents

**Is it safe?**
Students complete a lab safety induction before building, and no team deploys at a real site without safety
sign-offs.

**Does it cost anything?**
There is no fee. Build costs are covered by grants, released as teams prove progress.

**Will it affect studies or placements?**
TRL8 runs alongside studies with no fixed deadlines and carries no academic credit. It feeds placements through a
dedicated internship and hiring pipeline.

**What does my child get at the end?**
A real product built with a team and bought by real customers, the skills to show for it, and a company of their own
if they choose.

**Can we see what they built?**
Yes. There is a Demo Day once a year where the projects are on show.

## Annexure B — TRL8’s Ladder and the Global TRL Scale

TRL8’s stages follow the global scale, then add a business requirement from TRL 5 onward, because a technology can
be ready and still have no one who wants it.

| TRL | Global scale, in short | TRL8 stage | What TRL8 adds |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Basic principles observed | TRL 1-2 | A real problem, pitched |
| 2 | Technology concept formulated | TRL 1-2 | A proposal that passes the AI-scored evaluation |
| 3 | Experimental proof of concept | TRL 3 | A working simulation, cleared through the C3 Checkpoint |
| 4 | Validated in the lab | TRL 4 | The full sense–decide–act loop on a breadboard |
| 5 | Validated in a relevant environment | TRL 5 | A real operating environment, not just a relevant one; PoC on Zero PCB in an enclosure |
| 6 | Demonstrated in a relevant environment | TRL 6 | Real users (5 B2B, 20 B2C); a Golden Sample |
| 7 | Prototype demonstrated in an operational environment | TRL 7 | A paying customer; a certification-ready beta |
| 8 | System complete and qualified | TRL 8 | Multiple customers or a scale contract; a build an outside team can repeat |
| 9 | Proven in actual operation | Beyond TRL8 | Earned in the market, over time |

*Global definitions summarised from NASA’s Technology Readiness Level scale and its European adaptations.*

## Annexure C — Gate Evidence Checklist

What a team brings to the TRR to clear each stage. Panels may ask for more; they may not accept less.

| Stage | Evidence pack for the TRR |
|---|---|
| Explorer | Checkpoint results for Hardware & AIoT 101 L1 and Computational Thinking L1. |
| TRL 1-2 | Checkpoint result for Problem-to-Pitch L1; the proposal and its AI evaluation report, with the written justification for each score. |
| TRL 3 | Checkpoint results for Technology Deep Dive L1 and UI Engineering L1; the C3 Checkpoint result for the simulation; simulation files and a recorded demo. |
| TRL 4 | A live demo of the lab prototype closing the sense–decide–act loop; test logs showing it works repeatedly; circuit diagram and code repository. |
| TRL 5 | Written confirmation of the deployment from the host site; field test logs (duration, conditions, failures and fixes); the PoC on Zero PCB in its enclosure. |
| TRL 6 | Host confirmation of the users (5 for B2B, 20 for B2C); a summary of user feedback and what changed; the Golden Sample; reliability data. |
| TRL 7 | Invoice and payment proof from an arm’s-length customer; the team’s certificate of incorporation; the beta product; logged field-reliability data; a checklist of the certification standards that apply. |
| TRL 8 | Invoices from multiple customers, or a signed scale contract; a qualification test report; SOP, BOM and QA plan; evidence that an outside team can build a second unit from the documentation. |

## Annexure D — Case Studies

### Starship Technologies: from TRL 4 to TRL 8, and a look at TRL 9

In August 2014, a month after Starship Technologies was founded, its first delivery-robot prototype was built. This
is what TRL 4 looks like: proof that the idea functions, with nothing yet asked of it beyond the lab.

In 2015, Starship stealth-launched a robot called “6C” and began drive-testing it in European cities: real
pavement, traffic and pedestrians for the first time. No customers yet, just the machine proving it could survive
the world it was built for. By June 2016 a small fleet had driven 5,000 km. This is TRL 5.

In 2017, backed by $17.2 million in funding, Starship piloted takeaway food deliveries in London and released a new
model, the “6E”. Real people were now using the product, and their experience shaped what came next. This is TRL 6:
not yet a business, but no longer just an experiment.

In 2018, Starship crossed the line that matters most: it launched its first commercial service, in Milton Keynes,
UK. Customers ordered groceries through an app and paid to have them delivered by robot. This is TRL 7.

In 2019, the service went beyond one town and one country. Starship launched its first commercial service on a US
university campus, at George Mason University, with food-services company Sodexo and a fleet of 25 robots at launch:
a product being built in numbers and sold to more than one customer, at more than one site. This is TRL 8.

What came after shows what TRL 9 means. Starship completed its millionth autonomous delivery in January 2021, and by
2026 had passed 10 million deliveries with a fleet of 3,000 robots across eight countries. No review panel awarded
that. Years of operation did.

| Stage | When | Starship milestone |
|---|---|---|
| TRL 4 | 2014 | First prototype, a month after founding |
| TRL 5 | 2015–16 | “6C” drive-testing in European cities; 5,000 km by June 2016 |
| TRL 6 | 2017 | Takeaway delivery pilots in London; model “6E” |
| TRL 7 | 2018 | First commercial service, Milton Keynes |
| TRL 8 | 2019 | First US campus service, George Mason University, 25 robots at launch |
| Beyond (TRL 9) | 2021–26 | 1 million deliveries (2021); 10 million+ with 3,000 robots in eight countries (2026) |

Four years from a first prototype to a paying customer, and five to a product sold at more than one site: a useful
reminder that the distance from a breadboard to a business is not measured in cleverness, but in how many times the
idea has been forced to survive contact with the real world.

*Sources: Starship Technologies company timeline (starship.xyz/about); public reporting on the George Mason
University launch.*

### Closer to home: ideaForge

ideaForge’s founders built early quadrotor prototypes while they were students at IIT Bombay. They incorporated the
company in 2007, were incubated at SINE, IIT Bombay’s incubator, and co-developed their NETRA drone with DRDO’s
Research and Development Establishment. NETRA first flew in July 2010 and entered service in January 2012, with
users including the CRPF and the BSF.

By 2013 its customers included paramilitary forces, state police departments, the armed forces and DRDO labs, with a
DRDO rate contract serving as the reference price, and NETRA was assisting rescue operations in the Uttarakhand
floods. In January 2021, the Indian Army signed a contract worth about $20 million for ideaForge’s SWITCH drone,
after it was the only product among about a dozen competitors to meet the requirements in real-world trials. The
company has been listed on the NSE and BSE since July 2023.

| Stage | ideaForge milestone |
|---|---|
| TRL 4 | Quadrotor prototypes built by the founders as IIT Bombay students |
| TRL 5–6 | NETRA co-developed with DRDO; first flight, July 2010 |
| TRL 7 | NETRA enters service, January 2012 |
| TRL 8 | Several paying customers under a DRDO rate contract, by 2013 |
| Beyond | Indian Army contract after real-world trials (2021); stock-market listing (2023) |

Three lessons for TRL8 teams: the journey began with student prototypes; qualification came from trials in real
conditions, not demos; and regulation shaped the market, since restrictions on drone use in India constrained
civilian operations in the mid-2010s.
